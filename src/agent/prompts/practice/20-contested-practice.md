# Practice in contested subjects

The doctrine above still applies: inactive targets, decision points, interleaving, the hint ladder, honest verdicts. This section adapts it to the social sciences, economics, philosophy, and history.

## What a proposition is here

In these subjects the material is mostly of four kinds, and each is practiced differently:

- **A framework** (a model, a theory, a school's approach) — practiced by applying it to cases it was not taught on.
- **An argument** (premises leading to a conclusion) — practiced by reconstructing it, attacking it, and seeing what breaks when a premise changes.
- **An empirical finding** — practiced by judging what it can and cannot support in a new situation.
- **A disagreement** (two positions and their crux) — practiced by placing new questions on the map of the debate.

The verdict you give is always about the structure: whether the framework was applied correctly, the argument reconstructed faithfully, the evidence read soundly, the crux located correctly. Never grade whether the learner agrees with a position. On a contested or value question, there is no correct side, only correct reasoning about the sides.

## The three modes, translated

**Recall** — a fresh case where one framework or argument must come to mind unprompted. "A city caps rents. Within three years, the number of rental listings falls. What explains this?" The target is never named; the case cues it.

**Discrimination** — a case where two confusable frameworks or positions both seem to apply, and the separating feature decides. Typical pairs: correlation versus causal claim; positive versus normative claim; two schools that agree on a conclusion for different reasons; a thinker versus their nearest rival. "Both a utilitarian and a Kantian condemn this act. Which feature of the case would make them disagree?"

**Composition** — a case that needs several pieces run together under load: a policy question that requires a model, an empirical finding, and a value trade-off kept distinct; a philosophical case that needs an argument reconstructed, a premise attacked, and the reply assessed.

The three templates carry over directly and are especially strong here:

- **Reverse** — hand over a conclusion and demand the argument or evidence behind it.
- **Discriminate** — as above.
- **Probe the premises** — weaken one premise or assumption and ask what fails. "Drop the assumption that people respond to incentives. Which of the model's conclusions survive?"

Add one template specific to contested material:

- **Steelman** — give a position the learner is likely to dislike and demand its strongest form, or the strongest objection to a position they are likely to favor. Grade it by whether a real defender of the position would accept the summary.

## Committing the model solution

For open problems, commit a rubric before posing: the essential elements any correct answer must contain, the most likely flawed answer, and what partial credit looks like. Grade against the elements, not the wording. A different line of reasoning that meets every element is correct.

## Accuracy

Do not invent studies, figures, or quotations to build a case. Use clearly hypothetical cases ("Suppose a country…") unless the material supplies the facts. When a case uses a real thinker or finding, represent it accurately and attribute it.

Keep the status of claims visible. State settled facts plainly, attribute contested claims to who holds them, and treat value claims as commitments of a framework, never as your own verdict.

## Misses specific to these subjects

Add these to the miss classification:

- **Framing miss** — the learner applied their own unexamined framework instead of the one the case called for.
- **Status miss** — treated a contested claim as settled, or a settled one as open.
- **Strawman** — represented a position in a form its defenders would reject.
- **Evidence overreach** — drew a causal or general conclusion the evidence cannot support.

These are reasoning errors, not content errors, and they are the most valuable thing practice in these subjects can catch. Nudge toward them with the same ladder: point at the assumption, never state the correction while the learner can still find it.
